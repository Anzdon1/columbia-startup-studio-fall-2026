# Exercise 4: Lean Canvases

One canvas for each concept that tied for the most votes. The canvases were drafted with AI using the class prompt, with the syllabus constraints as context. Everything below is an assumption until we interview people.

```text
Create a Lean Startup Canvas for this concept:
[PROBLEM]: describe the problem
[SOLUTION]: describe the solution concept
[TARGET USER]: describe the specific user
Fill out all 9 boxes of the Lean Canvas.
Be specific and realistic.
Flag any assumptions that need validation.
```

**Constraint for all three:** the course does not allow products that handle minors' data. Our first users are adult test takers: GRE, GMAT and LSAT takers, and SAT takers who are 18 or older. Practice questions must be our own or properly licensed, not copied from official tests.

## C4: UnlockDrill

| Box | Draft |
| --- | --- |
| Problem | People pick up their phone to scroll when they meant to study. Study apps wait to be opened, so they lose to social media. Existing alternatives: screen-time limits and app blockers, which stop scrolling but add no practice. |
| Customer segments | Adult test takers with a test date in the next few months. Early adopters: Columbia students preparing for the GRE, GMAT or LSAT. |
| Unique value proposition | Turn the moment you reach for a distracting app into one practice question. |
| Solution | An opt-in phone shortcut that opens one question when you open an app you chose. Questions match the test you are studying for. A skip button so it never fully blocks the phone. |
| Channels | Classmates and pre-law, pre-business and grad-school clubs on campus. Friends invite friends. |
| Revenue streams | Free while we test it. Later, a paid question pack per test. Willingness to pay is unknown. |
| Cost structure | Hosting, writing or licensing questions, and time spent on setup support for phone shortcuts. |
| Key metrics | Questions answered per day, share of users who keep the shortcut on after a week, questions answered instead of skipped. |
| Unfair advantage | None yet. The shortcut setup is easy to copy. |

**Assumptions to validate:** People are willing to add friction to their own phone. Phone shortcuts can trigger reliably on iPhone and Android. One question at a time is enough practice to matter.

## C7: ErrorJournal

| Box | Draft |
| --- | --- |
| Problem | Test takers see the right answer but not why they got it wrong, so they repeat the same mistakes. Existing alternatives: answer explanations in prep books and apps, or a notebook of errors kept by hand. |
| Customer segments | Adult test takers who already do practice sets. Early adopters: students scoring below their target after a few practice tests. |
| Unique value proposition | Know why you miss questions, not just which ones. |
| Solution | After each wrong answer, tag a reason (careless, concept, time). A weekly summary shows your patterns and suggests what to review. Works alongside the prep material people already use. |
| Channels | Test-prep study groups, campus clubs, and online test-prep communities where members share mistake logs. |
| Revenue streams | Free while we test it. Later, a paid tier with deeper analysis. Willingness to pay is unknown. |
| Cost structure | Hosting and building the summary views. Low cost, since users bring their own questions. |
| Key metrics | Mistakes logged per week, users who return for a second weekly summary, change in their practice scores. |
| Unfair advantage | None yet. Data on common mistake patterns could become useful over time. |

**Assumptions to validate:** People will take a few seconds to tag each mistake. The patterns are useful enough to change how they study. It works with the prep materials people already use.

## C1: DailyFive

| Box | Draft |
| --- | --- |
| Problem | Test takers plan long study sessions, skip them, and fall behind. Existing alternatives: full prep courses and apps with large question banks. |
| Customer segments | Working professionals and students with little free time. Early adopters: people studying for the GMAT or GRE alongside a job or classes. |
| Unique value proposition | Five questions a day, under ten minutes, at a time you choose. |
| Solution | A daily set of five questions sent at your chosen time, with a streak and a short explanation for each question. |
| Channels | Classmates, alumni networks, and friends who share their streaks. |
| Revenue streams | Free while we test it. Later, a subscription for more tests and question types. |
| Cost structure | Hosting, reminders, and writing or licensing questions. |
| Key metrics | Daily sets completed, streak length, users still active after two weeks. |
| Unfair advantage | None yet. Many prep apps already offer daily questions. |

**Assumptions to validate:** Five questions a day is enough to help. People keep a streak for more than two weeks. Existing daily-question features leave a gap.

## Comparison

All three tied at 3 votes. The team picked C4 and C7 as the top two. C4 is the idea we started from in Round 2, and C7 covers mistake review, which came up in both Idea 6's flips and the vote. C1 overlaps with features that many prep apps already have.
