### Contradictions and what they might mean

- **Some renters used spreadsheets, while others relied on informal tools.** Zaynah, Jude, and Jackson used spreadsheets to compare options. Yuk Yi sent screenshots to her mother and kept information in chats, while Sean used Facebook Messenger. This may be because the number of listings, how many people were involved in the decision, and the amount of time available differed from person to person.
- **Online listings could be useful but were not always reliable enough.** Jude felt that the StreetEasy listings she saw generally matched the apartments in person. In contrast, Zaynah noticed AI-generated photos, and Yuk Yi was scammed by a listing on a site that claimed to be safe. This may depend on the platform, the type of housing, and what information the renter is trying to verify.
- **The amount of urgency was very different across searches.** Jude had about a week to find a place while staying in a hotel, and Zaynah lost listings that disappeared by the next day. Jackson searched across two to three months. Yuk Yi felt pressure but was sometimes able to ask a sublessor for extra time. This may be explained by differences in move-in deadlines, budget, and whether the renter was looking for a regular lease, student housing, or a sublet.

### Surprises

- The product problem is not simply "find more listings." Several participants already had many sources and instead struggled to know what information was missing or how to compare it.
- Personal networks were repeatedly important: Yuk Yi found her home through one, Sean trusted a friend's experience, and Jackson wanted access to current residents. This suggests that renter-to-renter context has value, while also raising moderation and verification questions.
- International and nontraditional renters face materially different paperwork and guarantor constraints.

### Overall read

Apartment hunting in NYC is difficult because renters have to consider many options, find missing information, and make decisions quickly. These interviews show that people have differring priorities, but they all want a clearer way to understand the apartments they are considering.

## Part B: AI Synthesis

### Patterns

**1. The search is time-consuming and pressure changes how renters decide.**

This appeared **strongly** in Zaynah, Jude, and Yung Yi; **moderately** in Jackson; and is not sufficiently documented in Sean's available excerpts. Zaynah spent about eight hours a day during a three-week search and often lost listings to faster applicants. Jude had about one week while paying for a hotel and preparing for school, then chose the apartment that approved her more quickly. Yung Yi spent two to three hours a day searching during August and said the mental pressure contributed to rushing into a scam. Jackson searched over two to three months, but still felt pressure from student-housing companies to decide quickly. The shared theme is not simply that searches take time; it is that timing can limit a renter's ability to compare options carefully.

**2. Renters manually assemble a comparison process from many tools.**

This appeared **strongly** in Zaynah and Jude; **moderately** in Jackson and Sean; and **differently** in Yung Yi. Zaynah's father used Excel to track at least 20 listings in three days. Jude used Excel before arriving in New York, then a Google Doc, WhatsApp, and tour videos. Jackson used a spreadsheet for notes, photos, and pros and cons. Sean described using Facebook Messenger instead of a spreadsheet. Yung Yi did not use a formal tracker; she sent screenshots to her mother and relied on chats. The evidence supports a fragmented process, but it does not support the claim that every renter wants or will use the same tracking tool.

**3. Renters struggle to know what is true about an apartment before committing.**

This appeared **strongly** in Jude and Yung Yi; **moderately** in Zaynah, Jackson, and Sean. After moving in, Jude encountered pests, a broken AC unit, and costs that were not clear before signing. Yung Yi found that photos did not show cleanliness or actual room size, and she was scammed by a fake listing on a platform that emphasized safety. Zaynah saw AI-generated listing images and wanted clearer information about features, contacts, and guarantor rules. Jackson wanted to know more about surrounding conditions and hear from current residents rather than sales staff. Sean said guarantor acceptance was unclear from a listing. These are related, but not identical, gaps: some concern the apartment itself, some concern the building or neighborhood, and some concern the trustworthiness of the source.

**4. Renters' constraints make a universal "best apartment" unlikely.**

This appeared **strongly** in all five interviews. Zaynah prioritized responsiveness, size, location, and clear rules. Jude prioritized laundry, avoiding a ground-floor apartment, price, and a guarantor a building would accept. Yung Yi needed a short-term arrangement compatible with her timeline and a sublessor whose guest policy fit her life. Jackson wanted student-specific housing close to Columbia, with usable amenities and a certain overall feel. Sean and his roommates weighed bathrooms and in-unit laundry against the lack of a separate living room. The commonality is not one shared set of priorities; it is the need to make trade-offs visible.

**5. Trust often came from people or affiliations, not just the listing platform.**

This appeared **strongly** in Yung Yi and Sean; **moderately** in Jackson; and is present but less central in Zaynah and Jude. Yung Yi ultimately found a place through a Columbia-affiliate network after a scam. Sean felt safer after getting a friend's videos and advice from someone who had lived in the building. Jackson wanted candid perspectives from current residents. Zaynah relied on word of mouth for neighborhood knowledge, and Jude's experience shows that an accurate listing photo did not guarantee reliable management information. This is not evidence that personal networks are always available or correct; it shows why renters sought another source of confidence.

### Five telling quotes

1. "Skip the broker and search StreetEasy directly — it's not as complicated as it seems." - Jude interview.
2. "Two to three hours a day looking at the website." - Yung Yi interview.
3. "A unit was already in my wall when we toured, but it turned out to be broken, so I had to replace it." - Jude interview.
4. "Start looking now." - Jackson interview.
5. "having somebody to consult that wasn't just trying to charge me money" - Sean interview.

All five quotations are copied verbatim from the available interview transcript material.

### Contradictions

- **Listings were sometimes accurate, but not reliably complete.** Jude said the StreetEasy listings she toured matched their photos, while Yung Yi found that photos could hide cleanliness and room size and was scammed by a fake listing. Zaynah also saw AI-generated images. This may reflect different platforms, housing types, and the difference between a photo accurately showing a room and a listing answering all of a renter's questions.
- **Some renters used formal trackers, while others did not.** Zaynah, Jude, and Jackson used spreadsheets or structured documents; Yung Yi relied on screenshots and conversations, and Sean used Messenger. This may reflect how many options they were managing, whether they were deciding with family or roommates, and personal tolerance for administrative work.
- **The pace of the search was not uniform.** Jude and Zaynah faced immediate deadlines and disappearing listings. Yung Yi felt pressure but could sometimes ask for extra time. Jackson had months to search, though he still experienced pressure from providers. This may be explained by different move-in dates, budgets, and housing models: regular leases, sublets, and student housing.

### Surprises

- A renter can feel reasonably confident about listing photos and still discover important problems after moving in. Jude's accurate-photo experience and later pest and AC issues show that visual accuracy is not the same as a complete picture of living conditions.
- The most trusted route was sometimes not the most polished platform. Yung Yi's final arrangement came through a Columbia-affiliate network, while Sean valued a friend with direct experience.
- Broker dissatisfaction appeared in Zaynah and Jude's interviews, but the available evidence does not show that every renter rejects broker help. It only shows that these renters did not find the help worth its cost or limitations.

### Segments

- **Independent market renters under a deadline:** Zaynah and Jude were comparing regular apartments quickly, with a large amount of information and application work to manage. Jude's international guarantor requirements are an additional constraint, not an assumption about every renter in this group.
- **Flexible-term renters:** Yung Yi was looking for a sublet because a full lease did not match a possible move. Her priorities included timing, roommate fit, guest rules, and safety when communicating with strangers online.
- **Student-housing renters:** Jackson chose a student-specific provider for convenience, proximity to campus, amenities, and a social housing environment. His search involved fewer options than the open rental market but still lacked candid resident information.
- **Roommate decision-makers:** Sean's available excerpts describe roommates weighing shared amenities and layout trade-offs. The available record does not provide enough detail to describe this segment beyond that joint decision process.

## Part C: Comparison

The AI synthesis made two things clearer than our own. First, it separated different information problems: an apartment can have accurate photos while still hiding poor living conditions, unclear fees, or an untrustworthy source. Second, it identified distinct renter groups instead of treating all NYC renters as one audience: people searching regular apartments under a deadline, people looking for flexible sublets, student-housing renters, and renters making decisions with roommates.
