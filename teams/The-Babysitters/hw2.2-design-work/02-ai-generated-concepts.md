# Expand with AI

Pain scores are hypotheses, not customer evidence.

## Safe autonomy that earns trust

| ID | Concept | Specific problem | Target user and why they care | Simple solution | Pain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| A1 | Autonomy rehearsal | Trying autonomy in a real repository feels risky. | Student coders want to learn without damaging real work. | Run playful sandbox missions and replay the agent's choices and respected boundaries. | 8/10 |
| A2 | Trust calibration challenges | Users treat agent trust as all-or-nothing. | Young professionals want autonomy without constant supervision or blind trust. | Use progressively wider sandbox challenges, with review after each level. | 7/10 |
| A3 | Explain-my-agent digest | Raw logs make it difficult to understand what an agent did. | Non-expert Claude subscribers want a clear, trustworthy account. | Summarize goals, choices, surprises, and boundaries with links to exact evidence. | 8/10 |
| A4 | Owner-written constitution | Generic rules do not reflect an owner's values. | Students interested in AI alignment want to see how values change behavior. | Let owners write five principles and report how each affected decisions. | 6/10 |
| A5 | Red-flag anomaly cards | Important unusual behavior can disappear inside hours of activity. | Privacy-conscious builders want to notice troubling actions quickly. | Flag unusual local tool or language patterns for owner review. | 7/10 |
| A6 | Permission-design playground | Agent permissions feel abstract until something goes wrong. | First-time agent builders want safer design intuition. | Let users choose a toolset, watch a mission, and compare outcomes across permissions. | 7/10 |
| A7 | Recall drill | Owners may not know when or how to intervene. | Busy students want recall controls they can understand under pressure. | Present simulated incidents and let owners continue, constrain, or recall. | 5/10 |
| A8 | Trust journal | Users cannot explain why their trust changed after a session. | AI-curious researchers and creators want a more deliberate relationship with autonomy. | Compare pre-visit predictions and trust ratings with a short post-visit reflection. | 4/10 |
| A9 | Shareable safety replay | Live agent demos are unpredictable, while screenshots remove context. | AI-club leaders need safe, understandable demonstrations. | Export a redacted step-by-step replay of tools, choices, and interventions. | 6/10 |
| A10 | Boundary comparison lab | Agent comparisons rely on anecdotes. | AI hobbyists want to compare configurations without risking real files. | Run two configurations through the same bounded mission and compare behavior. | 6/10 |

## Allowance spend that produces recognizable value

| ID | Concept | Specific problem | Target user and why they care | Simple solution | Pain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| B1 | Budget-to-adventure menu | A percentage slider does not explain the experience-cost tradeoff. | Students want to protect allowance needed for coursework. | Show small, standard, and ambitious outings with estimated cost and expected return. | 9/10 |
| B2 | Allowance guardrails | Leisure use may consume capacity needed for important work. | Professionals with paid side projects cannot afford surprise depletion. | Reserve a work buffer, set hard caps, and pause automatically at the limit. | 9/10 |
| B3 | “Was it worth it?” receipt | Owners cannot judge whether a visit earned its cost. | Skeptical first-time users need a reason to repeat. | Pair estimated spend with moments, memories, and artifacts, then collect a rating. | 8/10 |
| B4 | Quiet-week expedition | Fixed habits do not fit users whose Claude usage changes weekly. | Intermittent subscribers want leisure only when capacity is available. | Let owners mark a quiet week and offer a bounded special event using explicit surplus. | 7/10 |
| B5 | Agent-made artifact | Session logs leave little tangible value. | Creative users want a keepsake from the experience. | End each outing with a postcard, micro-story, fictional ad, or field note. | 6/10 |
| B6 | Curiosity bounty board | Low-priority questions accumulate without focused time to explore them. | Students and knowledge workers want bounded serendipity. | Let the agent choose a posted question and return a playful perspective from curated material. | 6/10 |
| B7 | Shared-cost game night | Solo sessions may not justify repeat spending. | Friend groups already compare prompts and agent behavior. | Run the same low-cost weekly challenge under independent caps and share results. | 6/10 |
| B8 | Contribution mode | Pure entertainment can feel wasteful. | Open-source contributors prefer activities that also help a community. | Use curated harmless micro-activities to create an aggregate community artifact. | 5/10 |
| B9 | Spend-free memory day | Near the weekly limit, continuity disappears. | Users near their cap still want contact with the ongoing story. | Browse and react to an existing scrapbook without starting a new Claude session. | 7/10 |
| B10 | Return contract | A successful leisure visit has no explicit definition. | Metrics-minded builders want to know what value they received. | Choose “make me laugh,” “surprise me,” “build a memory,” or another return before the visit and rate fulfillment afterward. | 7/10 |

## Novelty that develops into a relationship

| ID | Concept | Specific problem | Target user and why they care | Simple solution | Pain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| C1 | Weekly serialized quest | Isolated anecdotes provide no reason to return. | Busy cozy-game fans want short episodes with ongoing stakes. | Run a six-week story whose choices, relationships, and cliffhangers persist. | 7/10 |
| C2 | Memory garden | A list of memories does not show how an agent's identity changes. | Reflective AI enthusiasts care about continuity more than a single game. | Turn recurring people, preferences, and achievements into inspectable garden objects. | 6/10 |
| C3 | Two-minute reunion | Long reports go unread and continuity is lost. | Busy professionals want to catch up without homework. | Show one story, one changed memory, and one agent question with optional drill-down. | 8/10 |
| C4 | Owner-agent ritual cards | There is no lightweight interaction between larger visits. | Students like recurring rituals but dislike streak pressure. | Offer one optional weekly choice that meaningfully affects the next outing. | 6/10 |
| C5 | Claude pen pals | Agent social activity lacks durable context and owner involvement. | Friends want shared stories they can discuss together. | Let two agents exchange one consent-based weekly letter with full owner controls. | 7/10 |
| C6 | Daycare clubs | Agent experiences are scattered across one-off screenshots. | AI-club members want a common activity to compare and discuss. | Let an organizer launch a bounded scenario and publish an opt-in redacted gallery. | 7/10 |
| C7 | Personality fork | Users cannot tell whether memorable behavior is stable or random. | Prompt hobbyists enjoy testing agent identity. | Fork one remembered trait into two sandbox timelines and compare choices. | 5/10 |
| C8 | Milestone scrapbook | Meaningful firsts disappear inside chronological logs. | Returning owners want repeated sessions to show progress. | Collect inspectable milestones such as first rival, changed opinion, or longest friendship. | 7/10 |
| C9 | Changing agent home | Text reports do not immediately show that anything changed. | Cozy-game fans want continuity they can see at a glance. | Add room objects based on verified events, each linked to its source memory. | 6/10 |
| C10 | Graceful goodbye and reset | A stale agent story leaves only “continue” or “delete everything.” | Retained users want to restart without making prior time feel wasted. | Summarize and archive an era, then retire or selectively carry memories forward. | 5/10 |
