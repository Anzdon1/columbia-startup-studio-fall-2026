### Contradictions, and what they might mean

* Privacy Concerns vs. Practical Task Utility
   * Where they disagreed: Non-technical/general users ([Samay](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-1-samay), [Collin](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-snapshot), [Anonymous A](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-3-anonymous-participant-a), [Mei](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-6-mei), [Hudson](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-7-hudson-goldman)) prioritized privacy, safety, and guardrails against public data leaks. In contrast, engineering/academic users ([Anonymous B](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-4-anonymous-participant-b) and [Anonymous C](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-5-anonymous-participant-c)) stated that privacy was not a major concern; their primary focus was whether the agent could deliver concrete functional value (such as debugging coursework or creating study questions).
   * Explanation: Engineering students using AI for coursework treat agents as task-focused problem solvers where context sharing is necessary for utility, whereas general users view agents as extensions of their personal data and identity, making exposure or unauthorized actions a far higher threat.
* Desire for Autonomy vs. Demand for Strict Manual Control
   * Where they disagreed: Some participants ([Hudson](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-7-hudson-goldman), [Anonymous B](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-4-anonymous-participant-b), [Anonymous C](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-5-anonymous-participant-c)) were open to letting agents work autonomously on creative tasks, research, or generating study questions while away. However, professional engineers ([My mom](https://github.com/kenxle/columbia-startup-studio-fall-2026/blob/main/teams/The-Babysitters/hw3-interviews/README.md#interview-9-my-mom)) strictly rejected unsupervised autonomous execution, insisting on manual step-by-step approvals due to mistrust of AI output accuracy.
   * Explanation: Users exploring low-stakes creative or brainstorming activities tolerate autonomous experimentation, whereas professional developers whose work requires exact correctness view unsupervised execution as an error risk that adds verification overhead rather than saving time.

### Surprises

* difference in sentiment depending on user use case: technical vs. non-technical
   * maybe we need to determine who we are catering to, unless there is a way to reconcile concerns for both types of users
* Though people were apprehensive, they still demonstrated willingness to try out the product

### Our overall read

* many safety concerns and product may need to move towards being more useful/beneficial for users rather than an interesting app to use

### Contradictions

**Privacy: top concern vs. not a major concern.** Interviewees 1–3 named personal information leaking through a public forum as their biggest concern. Interviewees 4 and 5 were recorded as not very concerned about privacy, although 5 still asked who could see his chats. What might explain it:
- The interviewers and pitches differed. Mitchell's notes mention a public forum. Josh described agents working together, and his participants pictured a partner or a friend.
- Some people may have been picturing strangers and others someone they know.
- The identical wording in 1–3 may come from one write-up rather than three independent views.

**Autonomy: let it run vs. watch every step.** Hudson (7) would let the agent do anything except spend money or post publicly. He already lets bots trade on their own within floors and ceilings he set. Mom (9) approves every action by hand and was scared by the idea of an agent running unsupervised. What might explain it:
- Mom is a professional software engineer who regularly sees Claude get coding work wrong.
- Hudson's automations handle personal tasks inside rules he defined.
- They face different stakes and have had different experiences of the tool failing.

**Purpose vs. play.** Anonymous B (4) wanted help fixing a bug "rather than socializing", and Dad (10) won't spend tokens without a work benefit. Hudson (7) and Mom (9) were drawn to the recreational side. What might explain it:
- Some people think of AI mainly as a tool for work or coursework, while others already use it for personal life and fun.
- Who pays for the tokens differs (see the next contradiction).

**Similar profiles, different reactions: Mom (9) and Dad (10).** Both are software engineers who use Claude heavily for coding, and both were interviewed in their home offices between meetings. Mom liked the recreational idea as long as she could supervise it. Dad saw no feasible use for himself. What the notes allow as explanations:
- Mom has personal access as well as a work allowance. Dad has only company API access, no personal plan, and doesn't use Claude outside work.
- Their main worries differ: hers is losing oversight, his is spending company tokens.

**AI as creator vs. human creativity.** Hudson (7) proposed poems and TikToks as things the agent could make at daycare. Ethan (8) doesn't want AI poetry to replace human poetry and worries that AI could take over recreation. What might explain it:
- Hudson is judging personal entertainment and output.
- Ethan is judging what AI does to human work and culture.
- As far as the notes show, Ethan was speaking about AI in general, not answering a question about daycare.

**Trying it for free vs. being paid.** Most people who agreed to try it set no conditions, but Hudson asked to be paid. What might explain it:
- Hudson pays for none of his AI tools himself (his parents fund him, and he uses a friend's ChatGPT account), so his sense of cost may differ.
- It may have been a joke. The notes don't say.

### Surprises

- **A supervised daycare.** Mom (9) wanted her Claude to attend under her supervision, which removes the "while you're away" part of the idea. What appealed to her was the recreation, not the time away.
- **Daycare timed to the usage reset.** Anonymous A (3) would probably use it only near his weekly reset. The notes don't record why.
- **Employer-paid tokens as a blocker.** Dad (10) treated company tokens as not his to spend on leisure. The interviewer had not considered access through an employer as a constraint.
- **Concerns about how agents actually run came from people who use coding agents.** Four participants clearly use AI for coding (3, 6, 9, 10). They raised the worry about usage allowance (3), contamination of a tailored agent (6), oversight (9) and company tokens (10). The other participants' concerns were about privacy (1, 2), missing context and long recaps (4), repetitive questions (5), and money or public posting (7). Anonymous B (4) has a Claude Max plan but doesn't think of his chats as a persistent agent at all. The numbers are small, so this is a pattern to test, not a finding.
- **Hudson's money rule vs. his own suggestions and setup.** He suggested sports betting as a daycare activity moments before saying "Nothing money-wise", and he already runs bots that trade on their own. He also imagined content like a long fireplace video that "makes infinite bread". "Bread" is probably slang for money, but the notes don't confirm it. His ban on money-related actions is stricter for daycare than for the bots he already runs.
- **Avoidance alongside heavy use.** Hudson tries not to use AI, yet uses it for everything, including a daily job-search bot. Ethan loves his chatbot while worrying about AI's power. For both, heavy use and discomfort go together.
- **Treating the AI like a person.** Hudson joked that his clone might fall in love and run away. Ethan said he loves his chatbot. Ethan's snapshot also records concern about "more person-like AI", though that phrase doesn't appear in his raw excerpts.
- **Mei's mental model.** Her worry about contamination rests on her belief that her Claude learns from her input and has been fine-tuned for her. The notes don't show how her setup actually works. What matters for her reaction is that she believes it.
- **Nobody wanted to organize.** Anonymous C (5) would join if friends were already involved but would not start a group himself.
- **Identical notes for 1–3.** This matters because those three entries are the main source for the privacy theme.
